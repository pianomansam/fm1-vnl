# A '60s Jazz lead

Question: what makes the right-hand lines of lyrical late-'50s and '60s jazz piano recognizable, and which of those traits can become generative rules for VNL-1's lead without breaking Sam's rules? This follows the Impressionism lead (`docs/research/lead-impressionism-2026-10-29.md`, approved by ear) and sat beside the Neoclassical lead (not kept).

Scope: the style's lyrical single-note lines in ballads, jazz waltzes and modal vamps of the trio era, and its solos on standards. The block chords and the comping are not the target.

- The spike is on worktree branch `worktree-agent-a4802cdbc40dd46b4`, commit 3b387cd, on top of the Impressionism spike. Its style is `lead_style 4` (in `app/song/lead.c`).
- The renders are in `app/build/new/lead-60s-jazz-2026-10-29/` (renamed in the name scrub), described in that folder's `INDEX.md`.

## Summary

- The '60s jazz line is about **motif and time against the bar**. Impressionism's is about shape and color; the Neoclassical lead's was about architecture and breath. Its traits are:
  - one short motif, stated, then sequenced over the next chord and restated in new rhythmic positions;
  - phrases that start off the beat (after a rest on 1) and run over the bar line into the next chord;
  - arrivals on guide tones and colors (3rds, 7ths, 9ths, 13ths), not on roots;
  - rhythmic displacement: the same figure moved by an 8th or a beat, or grouped in threes against four;
  - 8th-note lines with the odd triplet "compressed" into the space before a target;
  - space between phrases;
  - a singing top voice.
- **Chromatic approach notes are central to the style, and Sam's no-rub rule forbids almost all of them.** A note a semitone from a chord tone rubs, and a chromatic approach note is, by definition, a semitone from its target. The spike has none (section 3 says what is lost and what stands in for it).
- Everything else fits the rules. The spike keeps all of them: **0 rubs** in all five renders and in 8 survey seeds.
- The spike sounds different from both other styles mainly in rhythm and continuity:
  - fewer notes: 1.8-2.5 per lead bar (current 2.3-2.8, Impressionism 2.4-3.1, Neoclassical 2.7-3.8);
  - mostly off the beat (33-64%), like Impressionism, but its onsets are 8ths, not 16th curls;
  - a rest of a quarter or more about every second bar (0.44-0.57 per lead bar);
  - about half of its statements are displaced (an 8th later, an 8th earlier, or a beat later).
- Its weak spot is **legato leaps**: 2-8 per song (Impressionism 0-2). The legal notes over a chord are sparse, so an 8th-note line that has to keep moving skips where the style would slide chromatically.
- Best approved lead: the **felt piano** (the style is piano writing; the struck line with a lifted top voice is its sound). Second: **sampled vibes** (the struck, ringing attack suits 8th-note lines). The flute works, but its long held targets sound more like Impressionism.
- Sam has to listen; numbers can't settle it.

## 1. What makes the lines recognizable

Sourcing note: the transcription-level analyses (a harmony book, a doctoral thesis, a chapter on displacement) are not freely readable online. I could confirm their scope and abstracts but not their examples. Claims marked "(listening)" are my own observations of the recordings, not cited analysis. Traits only: no melody, motif or lick from any recording is reproduced or stored.

### Motif first, developed

A thesis codifies "phrase-level melodic frameworks" in the style's solos on standards: the same frameworks reused across performances and filled with new lines, following the tune's guide-tone strands. An accent study describes the aim of a maximum degree of rhythmic contrast with the metrical pattern of the original harmonic succession. Style overviews describe improvisations that rely heavily on motivic development, melodic or rhythmic.

Lessons show chords outlined as lines (a triad over its maj7 chord; a triad on the 7th over a dominant, which gives a sus sound). The style's best-known modal vamp piece develops an opening motif (the inversion of the ostinato's falling 5th) over an unchanging maj7-to-9sus4 vamp. That is the closest the style comes to a lo-fi loop.

**Rule:** one motif per song (a rhythm cell plus a contour), stated, then restated over the next chord at the same chord function (sequence), varied in one way at a time.

### Phrases start off the beat and run over the bar line

Lessons note anticipations: an arrival tone placed early, on the "and" of 4, and sustained into the next bar. (Listening) In the jazz waltzes and ballads, phrases mostly start after a rest on 1 and resolve on or just after the next downbeat. The resolution falls in the next chord, not the one the phrase began in.

**Rule:** the motif starts on the "and" of 1, beat 2, the "and" of 2 or the "and" of 3. Its target lands in the next bar: on beat 1 (about 25% of songs), the "and" of 1 (about 40%), or beat 2 (about 35%).

### Rhythmic displacement

Displacement was demonstrated and described as deliberate practice in a well-known radio interview, and a book chapter treats it in Schenkerian terms. Accent studies name cross-accent among the style's accent processes. A 10-bar ballad form of the period is itself a displacement: the cycle loops back two bars before the ear expects it.

**Rule:** after the first statement, a restatement is displaced 65% of the time: an 8th later (30%), an 8th earlier (15%) or a beat later (20%). 15% of songs group the motif in dotted quarters (three 8ths against four: the hemiola).

### Guide-tone and color targets

Lesson examples resolve to chord tones (a walk up to the 7th of a dominant resolving to its 3rd) and use colors (#9 and b13 over a dominant). The frameworks are tied to guide tones. (Listening) The style's targets are rarely roots: 3rds and 7ths at cadences, 9ths and 13ths at held points in the ballads.

**Rule:** the target is a 3rd or 7th first, then a 9th, 13th or (over minor) 11th, the root last (on beats 1 and 3 a chord tone only). Each target sits a step or two from the last one, so the targets form a slow guide-tone line.

### Chromatic approach, enclosure, turns

The style walks up or down by half steps into chord tones, often with faster rhythm near the target. Enclosures (above and below the target) and turns (a quick flourish around a chord tone) are part of the same vocabulary.

**Rule:** see section 3. Only diatonic, legal approach notes remain.

### Triplet compression

8ths are compressed into a triplet that begins with a rest.

**Rule:** in 40% of songs with motion 50 or more, the two notes before the target become the last two of an 8th triplet.

### Space and singing tone

(Listening) The lyrical recordings breathe: the modal vamp piece and the ballads leave whole beats empty between ideas, and the waltz themes rest at their phrase ends. The top voice sings over the pianist's own voicings.

**Rule:** after a target, silence until the next statement (at least a quarter, usually half a bar). Over a struck lead, the line is louder than the keys.

## 2. How it differs from the Impressionism and Neoclassical leads

| | Impressionism (style 2) | Neoclassical (style 3, not kept) | '60s Jazz (style 4) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core idea | arabesque curls into long color notes | long periods in one breath | a motif sequenced and displaced against the bar |
| Note values | 16ths and triplets into held notes | quarters, halves, dotted values | 8ths (the odd triplet) into a target held a quarter to a half |
| Beat 1 | almost never struck (tied through) | struck (27-37%) | struck now and then by a target (6-20%); the line itself is off the beat |
| Bar line | a held note tied over it | phrases start on it | the line runs over it into the next chord's target |
| Repetition | one motif, varied once per head | antecedent and consequent | sequenced over each chord, re-timed (displacement) |
| Notes | pentatonic plus the mode's color note | modal, no leading tone | the chord-scale's legal notes; chord-tone skips where steps are not legal |
| Lost to the rules | whole-tone runs | semitone appoggiaturas | chromatic approach notes and enclosures |
| Rests | 6+ 16ths at each phrase end | only at the period's middle and end | a quarter or more about every second bar |

## 3. Fit with VNL-1's rules

| Trait | Fit | How it's kept legal |
|---|---|---|
| Motif and sequence | Fits | The contour is in legal scale steps, re-anchored on each chord. Chords are never changed. |
| Off-beat starts, runs over the bar line | Fits | Notes after the bar line are checked against the next chord (and its bass) when planned, and again when they sound. A note that rings over the bar line must be legal over both chords, else it is cut at the bar line. |
| Displacement | Fits | A shift of the whole statement. Onsets on beats 1 or 3 must be chord tones; a non-chord tone landing there moves to an adjacent chord tone or drops. |
| Guide-tone targets | Fits | Every target passes `legal()`; the maj7 over the root in the bass is refused (R25). |
| Triplet compression | Fits | Sample delay in thirds of a 16th (the Impressionism mechanism). |
| **Chromatic approach notes** | **Conflicts** | Not used. See below. |
| **Chromatic enclosure** | **Conflicts** | Only the diatonic version, where both neighbors are legal. |
| Space, singing tone | Fits | Velocities 64-84 (flute), 80-100 (felt piano), 92-99 (vibes): inside 25-110. |
| Simplest spelling | n/a in code | Docs use Gb, Bb, Db (not F#, A#, C#) except where the key needs sharps. |

### Chromatic approach notes against the no-rub rule (honest result)

A chromatic approach note is a semitone from its target. If the target is a chord tone, the approach note rubs against that chord tone in the keys or pad while it sounds. Sam's rule allows no semitone rubs, with no exception for weak beats or passing notes. So:

- **A half step into a chord tone, from below or above:** always a rub. Out.
- **A half step into a color tone (9th, 13th):** the note a semitone from a 9th or 13th is almost always a semitone from a chord tone too (in Cmaj7: Db rubs C, Eb rubs E; around the 13th A, Ab rubs G and Bb rubs B). Out.
- **Chromatic walk-ups of three or four notes** (the lesson's walk up to the 7th of a dominant): every inner note rubs something. Out.
- **Chromatic enclosure** (a half step above and below): out. A diatonic enclosure (a scale step above, a scale step below, then the target) is legal where both neighbors are, and the spike's "enclosure" contour does that.
- **A diatonic half step** (E to F in C): F is legal only where it is not a semitone from a chord tone. Over C it rubs E, so it is out there too. It can live where the chord lacks the other note (F over Dm7 or G7sus4).

What is lost: the slide into the target. That is half of what makes a '60s jazz piano line sound bebop-schooled rather than a modal melody. Without it, the line approaches its target by a scale step or a chord-tone skip, and where the legal notes are sparse it skips a 3rd or 4th (the legato leaps in section 5). The rhythm (off-beat starts, displacement, arrivals after the bar line) carries most of the identity that remains.

If Sam wants it back, the honest options are:

- (a) accept the loss (current spike);
- (b) a ruled exception: a chromatic passing note of an 8th or less, on a weak 8th, into a target a semitone away, never sounding while the chord tone it rubs is struck. Over percussive keys that is possible between their attacks. With the pad or sustained keys it would rub every time. It would need a ruling and an audible A/B.

## 4. Proposed rule set: the '60s Jazz lead

Numbers are the spike's (commit 3b387cd), to be tuned by ear.

**Unit.** 4 bars, twice per 8 (question, answer), anchored like the phrase planner (lead runs, 8-bar passes, a 4-bar remainder plays the answer). Each unit has two statements: one from bar 1, one from bar 3.

1. **Motif rhythm**, drawn once per song (onsets from the statement's bar; the last is the target, in the next bar):
   - 2, &2, 3, 4 → 1 (25%);
   - &2, 3, &3, &4 → &1, the late arrival (25%);
   - off-beat quarters &1, &2, &3, &4 → &1 (15%);
   - dotted quarters &1, 3, &4 → 2, three against four (15%);
   - a late start &3, 4, &4, 1 → 2 (20%).
2. **Contour**, drawn once per song: a fall into the target (35%), a turn (up a skip, down by steps: 25%), a rise (20%), or a diatonic enclosure (20%). It is counted in legal scale steps from the target, over the chord each note sounds in.
3. **Notes before the target:** 3 under motion 40; 3-4 from 40 to 65; 4 over 65. A note that would leap more than a 5th from the next one starts the line after it instead. No repeated notes.
4. **Target:** legal over its chord, a chord tone on beats 1 or 3. Cost: distance ×3; 3rd or 7th −3; 9th, 13th, (minor) 11th −3; root +8; 5th +3. An approach that would have to leap more than a minor 3rd into it costs 4 per semitone more. The first target sits near the key's tonic around E5. Each later one is near the last (bar 3's a step up in the question, two down in the answer), so the targets form a guide-tone line.
5. **Restatements:** the bar-3 statement, and every statement after the first unit, draw:
   - a displacement: none 35%, an 8th later 30%, an 8th earlier 15%, a beat later 20%;
   - a new contour (the next one) 25%;
   - one more note (bar-1 statements only) 30%.
6. **Close:** the answer's bar-3 statement rests on a chord tone (the root allowed) about a 3rd under the anchor, held 10 16ths.
7. **Triplet compression:** 40% of songs at motion 50+.
8. **Lengths:** each note held to the next onset (legato 8ths). The target is held 6 16ths (8 on beat 1), the close 10, never past the end of its bar. Then silence until the next statement.
9. **Velocity:** the line rises 64 → 76 into the target at 84; the close 70. A struck lead (the felt piano) +16. Vibes lifted to 88-99.
10. **Legality:** every note passes `legal()` over the chord it sounds in, and over both when it rings over the bar line. Chords are never changed. No chromatic approach notes.

**Not implemented, proposed next:**

- Longer lines at high motion: two statements chained without the rest (the trio recordings' 8th-note runs).
- A song-level arc: displacement grows through the song, as the "maximum rhythmic contrast" reading suggests.
- The 3/4 feel of the jazz waltzes: VNL-1 is in 4/4, so only the dotted-quarter hemiola stands in for it.
- Ruling (b) of section 3, if Sam wants to hear chromatic approach notes.

## 6. Which approved lead suits it

1. **Felt piano.** The style is piano writing, and its line is a struck, singing top voice. The spike lifts its velocity (80-100) so it sits over the Rhodes, which answers the Impressionism lead's felt-piano concern.
2. **Sampled vibes.** Struck and ringing: 8th-note lines with held targets sound natural on it.
3. **Flute.** It works, but its soft attack makes the displaced 8ths less audible than on a struck lead.
4. Knight, synth pluck, Emilyguitar, saw lead: not tried. Knight would be the truest timbre for the style, but here it is a keys instrument, and the lead must sit above the keys.

## 7. Concerns

- **Chromaticism lost** (section 3). This is the biggest one. Without it, some lines may sound "modal pop" rather than jazz.
- **Leaps.** 2-8 legato leaps per song, more than Impressionism. The cause is the sparse legal note set over some chords (Db Lydian, A Ionian). The Neoclassical spike had the same problem.
- **Sparseness.** 1.8-2.5 notes per bar is below the current lead. At low motion that is lyrical; it may sound thin over a busy kit.
- **Repetition.** The motif's rhythm is fixed per song, and VNL-1's chords loop every 4 bars. Displacement varies it, but the targets can still alternate between two notes for a whole block (song 1: F5 and Eb5).
- **Spike quality.** No host tests. Notes that can't be placed are dropped, so a statement can shrink to its target. The carried notes reuse the lead state across bar calls (one bar of look-ahead only).
